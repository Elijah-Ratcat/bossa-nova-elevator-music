[*A Snapshot of the History of Latin Artists at Carnegie Hall*](https://www.carnegiehall.org/-/media/CarnegieHall/Files/PDFs/About/Press-Resources/Nuestros-Sonidos/History-of-Latin-Artists-at-CH-Timeline.pdf?la=en). 4. Balliett, Whitney. “Bossa Nova, Go Home.” *The New Yorker*, December 1, 1962. [Publisher’s digitized text](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1962/12/01/1962-12-01-197-tny-cards-000076939). The online text displays November 24; the citation follows the print issue date.

Connects background associations and devaluation with international commercial adaptation while emphasizing the original songs’ craftsmanship. This is Treece’s interpretation. Locators follow the supplied excerpts and page footers.

# Further reading

Castro, Ruy. [“Música de elevador”](https://www1.folha.uol.com.br/colunas/ruycastro/2019/03/musica-de-elevador.shtml). *Folha de S.Paulo*, March 10, 2019. Describes hearing Miles Davis’s “So What” in an Ipanema elevator and recalls an undated conversation in which Jobim acknowledged his own music’s elevator circulation alongside Cole Porter and Chopin.

EMESP Tom Jobim. [“Entrevista com Hermeto Pascoal — Para mim música não é trabalho, é devoção”](https://emesp.org.br/noticias/entrevista-com-hermeto-pascoal-para-mim-musica-nao-e-trabalho-e-devocao/). September 2, 2009. Pascoal recalls a 1971 conversation with Jobim inside a New York elevator, saying Jobim was upset that bossa nova had become elevator music and wanted musical renewal.

Goldschmitt, K. E. [*Bossa Mundo: Brazilian Music in Transnational Media Industries*](https://academic.oup.com/book/32416). New York: Oxford University Press, 2019. Especially pp. 52–54, 72–75, 141, 149, and 155–159. Explains connections among adult music markets, background settings, the elevator trope, and later licensing and remix circulation.

Kasper, Jackson S. *Search for Authenticity: An Exploration into the Afro-Brazilian Jazz Genre*. MA thesis, Truman State University, 2022. Especially pp. 53–60 and 64. Examines how American media presentation, commercial saturation, and background use contributed to bossa nova’s elevator-music reputation. Its interpretation draws in part on Goldschmitt and other scholarship.

Lees, Gene. [“Bossa Nova—Anatomy of a Travesty”](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Music/DownBeat/60s/63/DB%201963-02-14.pdf#page=20). *Down Beat*, February 14, 1963, pp. 22–24, especially p. 24. Reports Jobim’s objections to commercial distortion and his defense of bossa nova as music deserving attentive listening.

Ringel, David. [“Tom Jobim no paredão”](https://www.jobim.org/jobim/handle/2010/10363). Interview/article catalogued as 1970, pp. 28–32, especially p. 30. Instituto Antonio Carlos Jobim archive, item 2010/10363. Jobim reports hearing “Casinha Pequenina” as background music inside a U.S. elevator.

Seifrit-Griffin, Stacie. [“Behind the Scenes with John Landis”](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-film-preservation-board/documents/Interview_John-Landis.pdf#page=8). Library of Congress. Interview conducted spring 2021, PDF p. 8. Landis recalls the cut from the converging pursuit to the elevator in *The Blues Brothers*; the interviewer highlights the music and the held shot.

Sejkora, Markus. [“‘Purists Will Wince’: Constructing Authenticity During the Bossa Boom”](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/9059273.pdf). *Ensayos. Historia y teoría del arte* 25, no. 40 (2021): 59–79, especially pp. 78–79. Examines how critics and promoters used authenticity to judge bossa nova, including the cultural authority claimed through both criticism and sympathetic advocacy.
